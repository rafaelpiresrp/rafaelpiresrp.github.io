Breakeven employment growth in Brazil

The pace of job creation that keeps unemployment stable has fallen by a third since 2013 and picked up again in 2026.

Rafael Pires · October 2026

Highlights

  • The breakeven is lower. Brazil needs about 60,000 new jobs a month to keep the unemployment rate stable. Its demographic component fell from 100,000 in 2013 to 65,000, because the population aged 14 and over now grows about half as fast as it did then.
  • Late 2025. Job creation slowed from 155,000 to 97,000 a month, in averages over 12 months, and unemployment still fell by about 1 percentage point a year, because lower participation pushed the breakeven close to zero.
  • 2026. Participation stopped falling and the breakeven went back to 60,000. With job creation at 88,000 a month, the gap over the breakeven narrowed to 28,500, and the decline in unemployment over 12 months slowed to 0.3 percentage point.
  • Rule of thumb. Every 10,000 jobs a month above the breakeven, sustained for a year, lowers the unemployment rate by about 0.1 percentage point.
  • What to watch. If participation grows again at its 2019 pace, job creation of 90,000 a month will only keep unemployment stable.

Reading the monthly number

Every month, the PNAD Contínua survey shows how many more people are employed in Brazil. On its own, that number says little about how tight the labor market is, because its effect on unemployment also depends on how many people join the labor force over the same period. A gain of 80,000 jobs a month can lower unemployment in one year and raise it in another.

A more useful reference is the breakeven, that is, the number of jobs the economy must add each month to keep the unemployment rate where it is. The Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco publishes this measure for the United States (Petrosky-Nadeau and Stewart, 2024). In this note I compute it for Brazil with monthly PNAD data from March 2012 to August 2026, show how the gap between job creation and the breakeven translates into changes in unemployment, and split the breakeven into a demographic part and a part tied to labor force participation. The calculations are in Box 1.

Box 1: How the breakeven is computed

Let E be the number of employed people and L the labor force, so that the unemployment rate is u = 1 − E/L. For u to stay unchanged from one month to the next, employment has to grow by 1 − u times the increase in the labor force. That is the breakeven:

Bt = (1 − ut−1) ΔLt(1)

I call the difference between job creation and the breakeven the gap, Gt = ΔEt − Bt. The gap determines the change in the unemployment rate exactly,

Δut = −Gt / Lt(2)

and over twelve months an average gap G changes the rate by about

ut − ut−12 ≈ −1200 Gt / Lt(3)

percentage points. With a labor force of 109 million people, that is 0.011 percentage point for every thousand jobs a month. Since the labor force equals the population aged 14 and over, N, times the participation rate, p, the decomposition ΔLt = pt−1ΔNt + NtΔpt holds exactly, and the breakeven can be written as

Bt = (1 − ut−1) pt−1ΔNtdemographics + (1 − ut−1) NtΔptparticipation(4)

The first term is the number of jobs needed to absorb population growth with participation held constant. The second reflects people entering or leaving the labor force.

A decade of cycles

In the 2015 and 2016 recession, employment fell by 129,000 a month on average while the labor force kept growing, and unemployment rose from 6.6% to 12.1%. In 2020 employment fell by 688,000 a month, but the labor force also shrank, by about 480,000 a month, as many people stopped looking for work. Unemployment rose 3.1 percentage points; with participation held constant, the same losses would have raised it by about 9 points.

In 2021 and 2022 those people returned to the labor force, which lifted the breakeven to 220,000 a month. Job creation was even stronger and unemployment fell more than six points. The gap measured over 12 months has been positive since July 2021 and took unemployment to 5.1% in December 2025, the lowest level in the series (Table 1).

The decline of the demographic component

In 2013, Brazil needed about 100,000 jobs a month just to keep up with growth in the population of working age, and today it needs about 65,000 (Chart 2). The population aged 14 and over grew 1.40% in the 12 months to March 2013 and 0.76% in the 12 months to August 2026 (Chart 3). The cohorts now reaching working age were born after fertility fell and are smaller, and IBGE projects that the total population will stop growing in 2041 (IBGE, 2024). Participation has no clear trend: outside the pandemic it has stayed between 61.4% and 63.6% since 2012, and it stood at 62.2% in August. It drives the swings in the breakeven from one year to the next, while the decline over the decade comes from demographics.

The breakeven in 2025 and 2026

In the second half of 2025, job creation slowed from 155,000 a month in August to 97,000 in December (Chart 4). Unemployment kept falling by about 1 percentage point a year because participation declined and took the breakeven from 63,000 to close to zero, so the gap stayed between 90,000 and 100,000 a month.

In 2026 participation stopped falling, and its contribution to the breakeven went from −66,000 to −5,000 a month, which brought the breakeven back to 60,000. Job creation dipped to 62,000 in June and recovered to 88,000 in August. Between December and August the gap narrowed by about 70,000 a month, to 28,500, and 61,000 of that came from the higher breakeven. As a result, the decline in unemployment over 12 months slowed from 1.1 percentage points in December to 0.3 point in August.

A rule of thumb for the monthly number

Equation (3) turns a scenario for job creation into a path for unemployment, given an assumption for the breakeven. Since the labor force changes slowly, the conversion has barely moved since 2013 (Chart 5): 10,000 jobs a month above the breakeven, sustained for a year, lower unemployment by about 0.1 percentage point. Table 2 applies the rule to job creation of 90,000 a month, close to the latest reading. If participation stays flat, unemployment falls another 0.3 point over 12 months; if participation grows as it did in 2019, unemployment stops falling; at the 2024 pace, it rises 0.3 point.

Implications

With the demographic component near 65,000, a pace of job creation that only kept unemployment stable in 2013 is now enough to lower it. Comparing current numbers with the averages of the last decade therefore tends to understate how tight the labor market is.

Part of the decline in unemployment in recent years came from slower growth of the labor force. For wages, the source of that decline matters little, since wages respond to how scarce available workers are. It does matter for how long unemployment can stay low, because a low rate can now be sustained with less job creation than in the past.

The gap also helps anticipate unemployment, since the rate only goes back up when the gap turns negative. In August it was 28,500 a month, less than a third of its December level. If participation keeps recovering, the breakeven should rise further and unemployment may stop falling even with stable job creation.

Data and caveats

I use the monthly PNAD Contínua series for the population aged 14 and over, the labor force and employment, from March 2012 to August 2026. The monthly figures come from the survey’s rolling quarters, so flows are shown as averages over 12 months, which remove seasonality, and comparisons use full years or windows of 12 months. The unemployment rate is recomputed as 1 − E/L so that the identities in Box 1 hold exactly. PNAD employment includes informal work, so these numbers are not comparable with the Novo CAGED count of formal jobs. The breakeven computed here looks backward and only picks up changes in participation after they happen. A version that looks ahead would combine IBGE population projections with assumptions for participation by age group.
